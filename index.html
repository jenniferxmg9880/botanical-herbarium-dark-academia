A Note Before You Begin

Every student of Herbology learns the same lesson twice: once from a lecture, and once from a flower that did not forgive inattention. The specimens catalogued below were chosen for their charm as much as their cruelty — each is or has been grown in ordinary gardens, admired by ordinary people, who did not know what they were tending.

Click or tap a specimen to turn it over. Read the field notes. Touch nothing in the actual greenhouse.

— Prof. M. Ravenwood
The Catalogue

Seven Specimens, Pressed & Labelled

No. 01
Brugmansia spp. Angel’s Trumpet

turn for field notes

Brugmansia spp.

Description

Pendulous, trumpet-shaped blooms up to a foot long, drooping from woody shrubs. Sweetly fragrant by night, when the flowers open fully to lure moths.

Toxicity

Every part contains tropane alkaloids — scopolamine, atropine, hyoscyamine. Ingestion causes delirium, hallucination, paralysis of the gut, and in sufficient dose, death.

Field Notes

Old apothecaries called it a component of “flying ointments,” rubbed onto skin rather than swallowed. Witches, allegedly, did not fly so much as dream that they did.

No. 02
Nerium oleander Oleander

turn for field notes

Nerium oleander

Description

A tidy, evergreen shrub with narrow leaves and clusters of five-petaled pink, white, or red flowers, planted along roadsides for its indifference to drought and neglect.

Toxicity

Every part — leaf, stem, sap, smoke of burnt wood — carries cardiac glycosides. A single leaf, chewed by a child or a curious pet, has been enough to kill.

Field Notes

Its beauty is precisely the trap: municipal planters full of a poison ranked among the deadliest ornamentals grown anywhere in the world.

No. 03
Atropa belladonna Deadly Nightshade

turn for field notes

Atropa belladonna

Description

Small, bell-shaped purple-brown flowers give way to glossy black berries, sweet enough to tempt a child and dangerous enough to end that child’s afternoon in a hospital.

Toxicity

Atropine is the namesake alkaloid, once dosed deliberately to dilate the pupils. In excess it brings racing pulse, fever, hallucination, coma.

Field Notes

“Bella donna” — beautiful woman. Renaissance ladies dropped its juice into their eyes for a wide, dark, fashionable stare, and paid for the fashion in blurred vision.

No. 04
Digitalis purpurea Foxglove

turn for field notes

Digitalis purpurea

Description

A tall biennial spire lined with dozens of speckled, thimble-shaped bells, rising from cottage gardens and woodland edges alike.

Toxicity

Digoxin and related cardiac glycosides slow and disrupt the heart. The margin between a therapeutic dose and a fatal one is famously narrow.

Field Notes

Medicine and poison from the same leaf: digitalis remains a treatment for certain heart conditions, prescribed in doses measured to the milligram.

No. 05
Aconitum napellus Wolfsbane · Monkshood

turn for field notes

Aconitum napellus

Description

Deep violet flowers shaped like a monk’s hood, arranged along a stout, upright stem in cool, damp gardens and mountain meadows.

Toxicity

Aconitine can absorb through unbroken skin. Numbness, paralysis, and cardiac arrest follow — among the most lethal alkaloids in the plant kingdom.

Field Notes

Named for wolves and worn by monks alike in folklore; arrow-tips were once dipped in its root. Garden gloves are not a suggestion here — they are policy.

No. 06
Convallaria majalis Lily of the Valley

turn for field notes

Convallaria majalis

Description

A gentle arch of small white bells, achingly fragrant, spilling from broad green leaves in shaded corners of temperate gardens each spring.

Toxicity

Convallatoxin and related glycosides affect the heart much as foxglove’s do. The water in a vase of cut stems becomes dangerous in its own right.

Field Notes

A traditional wedding flower, prized for meaning “return of happiness.” No bouquet in this academy’s history has ever been eaten, and it should stay that way.

No. 07
Colchicum autumnale Autumn Crocus

turn for field notes

Colchicum autumnale

Description

Pale lilac, crocus-like blooms that rise from bare soil in early autumn, with no leaves in sight — those appeared and vanished back in spring.

Toxicity

Colchicine resembles no antidote known to this academy’s infirmary. It has repeatedly been mistaken, fatally, for saffron crocus and wild garlic by foragers who did not look closely enough.

Field Notes

A plant that hides its leaves is a plant worth a second look. Sometimes what a specimen omits tells you more than what it shows.
